Chapter 01

IC engine vehicles

An internal combustion engine turns the chemical energy in fuel into crankshaft torque. The electronics decide how much air and fuel enter each cylinder, when the spark fires, and how the exhaust is cleaned.

Reference: Bosch, Automotive Electrics and Automotive Electronics, 5th ed., pp. 13–31 (Motronic, EDC) and pp. 310–353 (engine sensors). Updated for 2026.

The four-stroke cycle

One complete cycle takes two crankshaft revolutions (720°). Each cylinder fires once per cycle, so a four-cylinder engine fires every 180° of crank rotation.

Engine management system

The Engine Control Module (ECM) runs a sense–compute–actuate loop every few milliseconds, synchronised to crank angle. It reads air mass, speed and temperatures, looks up calibration maps, applies closed-loop corrections and drives power stages.

Fuel control

Base injector pulse width comes from air mass per cylinder divided by the target air–fuel ratio. Corrections are added for cold start, transients, battery voltage (injector dead time) and the lambda feedback trim.

Spark control

Ignition advance comes from a map indexed by speed and load, giving peak cylinder pressure around 10–15° after TDC. The knock controller retards individual cylinders when the knock sensor detects detonation.

Air control

With drive-by-wire, the pedal is only a request. The ECM converts it to a torque demand and positions the throttle plate with an H-bridge motor, checked by two redundant position sensors.

Emissions

A three-way catalyst converts CO, HC and NOx together only in a narrow window around λ = 1. The upstream lambda sensor closes the fuel loop; the downstream sensor monitors catalyst health for OBD.

Lambda in one line

λ = actual AFR ÷ stoichiometric AFR. Stoichiometric AFR is 14.7:1 for petrol, about 14.5:1 for diesel and 17.2:1 for CNG. λ < 1 is rich (excess fuel), λ > 1 is lean (excess air). Use the calculator in Chapter 11 to convert.

Revise in 60 seconds

  1. Four-stroke cycle = 720° crank; one power stroke per cylinder per cycle.
  2. Crank sensor gives speed and angle; cam sensor identifies the compression stroke.
  3. Fuel mass = air mass ÷ target AFR, then corrected by lambda feedback.
  4. Spark advance comes from an rpm × load map; knock control retards it.
  5. TWC needs λ ≈ 1. Diesel runs lean and needs DPF + SCR instead.
  6. Electronic throttle: pedal → torque request → throttle position, with redundant sensors (safety relevant).

Chapter 02

Electric vehicles

An EV replaces the engine, fuel system and most of the gearbox with a high-voltage battery, an inverter and an electric motor. The electronics now manage energy in both directions: out of the battery to drive, and back into it during braking and charging.

BEV powertrain and power flow

VCU

The vehicle control unit interprets the pedal, gear selector and brake, and decides how much torque to request from the inverter. It also blends regen with friction braking (with the ESC) and coordinates charging.

PDU and precharge

Contactors connect the pack to the HV bus. The inverter's DC-link capacitor is first charged through a precharge resistor, otherwise closing the main contactor would cause an inrush that welds its contacts.

Inverter

Six power switches (IGBT or SiC MOSFET) chop the DC bus into three-phase AC with PWM at roughly 8–20 kHz. Frequency sets motor speed; current amplitude and phase set torque.

DC-DC converter

Replaces the alternator. It steps the HV bus down to about 14 V to run every low-voltage ECU and recharge the 12 V battery.

How a PMSM makes torque

Three stator windings sit 120° apart. Feeding them with three sine currents 120° apart in time creates one magnetic field of constant strength that rotates. The permanent-magnet rotor follows that field. Torque depends on the angle between the stator field and the rotor field, called the load angle, and peaks at 90°.

Field-oriented control in four steps

  1. Measure two phase currents and the rotor angle (resolver or encoder).
  2. Clarke transform: three phase currents → two stationary-axis currents (α, β).
  3. Park transform: rotate by rotor angle → d (flux) and q (torque) currents. These are DC values that PI controllers can regulate.
  4. Inverse transforms and space-vector PWM produce the switch commands. Above base speed, negative id weakens the field so the back-EMF stays below the DC bus voltage.

Hybrid drive concepts

A hybrid has two energy sources, fuel and a battery, and at least one electric machine. The book classifies them by how the engine's power reaches the wheels. In a series hybrid the engine only drives a generator and the wheels are driven electrically. In a parallel hybrid engine and motor both drive the wheels mechanically and their torques add. A power-split hybrid uses a planetary gear to send part of the engine power mechanically and part electrically, which works like a continuously variable transmission.

Regenerative braking needs cooperation with the brake system: the brake ECU fills in the friction torque the motor cannot provide (at low speed, a full battery, or during ABS intervention) so the driver feels one consistent pedal. Today's brake-by-wire units do this blending without the driver noticing.

Battery and BMS

A pack is cells in series (to raise voltage) and parallel (to raise capacity). A 96S pack of NMC cells sits around 350 V nominal; 800 V platforms use roughly 192S. The BMS keeps every cell inside its safe window.

  • Measure: every cell voltage, pack current, module temperatures, insulation resistance.
  • Estimate: state of charge (coulomb counting corrected by OCV and Kalman filters), state of health, power limits.
  • Protect: open contactors on over/under-voltage, over-current, over-temperature or isolation fault.
  • Balance: passive (bleed resistors) or active (move charge) to keep cells matched.
  • Thermal: request heating or cooling from the thermal system.
  • Communicate: charge/discharge limits to the VCU and the charger.

Lithium-ion cells charge with constant current until the cell reaches its maximum voltage, then constant voltage while current tapers. That is why 10–80 % is fast and the last 20 % is slow.

BLDC motors and six-step commutation

A brushless DC motor is a permanent-magnet motor whose windings are switched electronically instead of by a commutator. Three Hall sensors, 120° apart, give the rotor position in one of six 60° sectors. For each sector the controller energises two of the three phases (one high, one low, one floating), so the stator field always leads the rotor by 60–120° and pulls it round. Speed is set by PWM duty on the high-side switch. It is simpler than FOC but causes a torque ripple at every step; that is why traction motors use sinusoidal FOC, while pumps, fans and blowers often use six-step.

Regulations and type approval for electric vehicles

Before an EV can be sold it needs type approval: proof that the vehicle and its battery meet electrical-safety, battery-safety, EMC and energy-consumption rules. Electrical safety (vehicle)

  • Isolation resistance ≥ 100 Ω/V for DC and ≥ 500 Ω/V for AC high-voltage circuits, monitored continuously by an insulation monitoring device.
  • Touch protection: live parts behind barriers rated IPXXD inside the cabin (1 mm wire) and IPXXB elsewhere (finger).
  • HVIL (high-voltage interlock loop): a low-voltage loop through every HV connector; opening a connector is detected before its power pins separate.
  • Orange cabling, warning labels and a manual service disconnect.

REESS tests in R100 Rev. 3

  • Vibration (7–50 Hz sweeps, 3 h), thermal shock (+60 / −40 °C, 5 cycles), mechanical shock, crush (≥ 100 kN).
  • Fire resistance (petrol pool or, new in Rev. 3, LPG burner).
  • External short circuit (≤ 5 mΩ), overcharge, over-discharge, over-temperature, and the new overcurrent test.
  • Pass: no fire, explosion, rupture, electrolyte leakage and, new in Rev. 3, no venting; batteries start fully charged. A thermal-propagation warning to occupants is also required.

Revise in 60 seconds

  1. Drive: battery → PDU → inverter → motor → reducer. Regen reverses the flow.
  2. AC charging goes through the on-board charger; DC fast charging bypasses it.
  3. Precharge prevents inrush into the DC-link capacitor and protects the contactors.
  4. Three 120° currents produce one rotating field; torque ∝ sin(load angle).
  5. FOC: Clarke → Park → PI on id/iq → inverse Park → SVPWM.
  6. BMS: measure, estimate SOC/SOH, protect, balance, thermal, communicate.
  7. BLDC six-step: Hall sector → two phases driven, one floating; FOC for smooth traction torque.
  8. Type approval: R100 Rev. 3 (M, N), R136 (L), GTR 20; India AIS-038 Rev. 2 and AIS-156; isolation ≥ 100 Ω/V DC, 500 Ω/V AC.
  9. Hybrids: series (engine only generates), parallel (torques add; P0–P4 motor positions), power-split (planetary gear, e-CVT).

Chapter 03

Electronic control units

A modern car carries anywhere from 30 to over 100 ECUs. Each one is a small embedded computer that reads inputs, runs control software and drives outputs, and they are grouped into functional domains.

Reference: Bosch, Automotive Electrics and Automotive Electronics, 5th ed., pp. 152–207 (system architecture, mechatronics, electronic components, control units). AUTOSAR and zonal content is newer than the book.

ECU explorer

Inside an ECU

Almost every ECU follows the same hardware pattern, whether it controls a window or a steering rack. What changes is the size of the microcontroller, the power stages and the safety mechanisms.

Inside the microcontroller

The microcontroller is where the ECU's software meets the physical world through on-chip peripherals. The same handful appears in every automotive MCU, from a small Arm Cortex-M in a seat module to a multicore AURIX in a brake controller.

AUTOSAR Classic software stack

AUTOSAR separates application logic from hardware so the same software component can move between ECUs and suppliers. Application components talk only to the RTE; the basic software (BSW) does the rest.

From many ECUs to zones

The industry is moving from one ECU per function (distributed) to domain controllers and now to zonal architecture: a few powerful central computers plus zone controllers that collect the I/O in each physical corner of the car over Ethernet. Benefits are shorter wiring harnesses, less weight and software that can be updated over the air in one place.

Revise in 60 seconds

  1. ECU = power supply + input conditioning + MCU + output drivers + transceivers.
  2. Domains: powertrain, chassis & safety, body & comfort, ADAS, infotainment & connectivity.
  3. ESC, EPS and airbag ECUs are typically ASIL D; infotainment is usually QM.
  4. AUTOSAR Classic: SWC → RTE → BSW (services, ECU abstraction, MCAL) + complex drivers.
  5. Trend: distributed → domain → zonal + central compute.

Chapter 04

Sensors & actuators

Sensors turn a physical quantity (speed, pressure, oxygen content, yaw rate) into an electrical signal the ECU can use. Actuators do the reverse: they turn the ECU's output into force, motion, flow or heat. Every control function in the car is a loop between the two.

Reference: Bosch, Automotive Electrics and Automotive Electronics, 5th ed., pp. 208–359 (sensor basics, measuring principles, sensor types, actuators). Diagrams redrawn; updated for 2026.

Where the intelligence sits

A sensor's raw element produces a tiny, nonlinear, temperature-dependent signal. Somewhere between the element and the ECU's software it has to be amplified, linearised, digitised and checked. The integration level says how much of that work happens inside the sensor housing. The more that moves into the sensor, the more robust the signal on the wire, and the less the ECU has to know about the physics.

Wheel-speed sensors: passive vs active

ABS, ESC, traction control and navigation all depend on four wheel-speed signals. A passive (inductive) sensor is a magnet and a coil facing a steel tooth wheel: each passing tooth changes the magnetic flux and induces a voltage. Both amplitude and frequency rise with speed, so at walking pace the signal disappears into noise. An active sensor uses a Hall or magnetoresistive IC that reacts to the field itself, not its rate of change. It outputs a clean two-level current at any speed down to standstill, which is why active sensors have been almost universal since the late 1990s.

Lambda sensors: two-step vs wide-band

Both types use a zirconium-dioxide ceramic that conducts oxygen ions when hot. A two-step (switching) sensor compares exhaust oxygen with reference air and produces a Nernst voltage that jumps at λ = 1: it can only say "rich" or "lean". A wide-band sensor adds an oxygen pump cell. The control electronics pump oxygen into or out of a tiny diffusion gap to hold it at λ = 1, and the pump current needed is a continuous measure of λ from very rich to pure air. Diesel and lean-burn engines need the wide-band type.

Actuators and power stages

Electromagnetic

Solenoid valves (injectors, ABS valves, transmission shift valves) and relays. The ECU drives the coil with a low-side switch; a free-wheeling path absorbs the turn-off spike. Peak-and-hold current control opens fast, then holds with less power.

Piezo

Stacked piezo ceramics expand in microseconds when charged. Used in diesel and GDI injectors for several precise injections per cycle. Driven with a high-voltage charge/discharge stage, not a simple switch.

Electric motors

Brushed DC for windows, seats and wipers, driven by H-bridges. Brushless DC/PMSM for pumps, fans, EPS and brake boosters, driven by three-phase bridges with FOC, the same principle as the traction inverter.

Smart power switches

2007: relays and fuses in a central box. 2026: smart high-side switches and eFuses with current sensing, programmable trip curves and diagnostics over SPI, placed in zone controllers. They are the actuators of the power network itself (Chapter 05).

By-wire actuation. Electro-hydraulic brake boosters and integrated brake units already decouple the pedal from braking force, enabling regen blending. Steer-by-wire and electromechanical brakes remove the mechanical or hydraulic fallback entirely, so they need redundant sensors, power supplies and ECUs (ASIL D, fail-operational).

Revise in 60 seconds

  1. Integration levels: raw element → + conditioning → + A/D and digital output → smart sensor with bus.
  2. Passive wheel-speed sensor: amplitude and frequency ∝ speed, fails near standstill. Active: Hall/MR, 7/14 mA current signal, works at 0 km/h.
  3. fsensor = teeth × wheel revolutions per second.
  4. Two-step lambda jumps at λ = 1 (rich ≈ 0.8 V, lean ≈ 0.1 V); wide-band pump current is continuous through 0 at λ = 1.
  5. MEMS: capacitive accelerometers, Coriolis gyros, piezoresistive pressure.
  6. ADAS sensor set: ultrasonic, 77 GHz radar (now 4D imaging), camera, lidar, interior monitoring.

Chapter 05

Vehicle electrical system

Every ECU, lamp and motor in the car runs from the low-voltage network: a battery, a generator to refill it, a starter, the wiring between them and the logic that keeps the energy balance positive. In an EV the alternator and starter disappear, but the 12 V (or 48 V) network stays.

Reference: Bosch, Automotive Electrics and Automotive Electronics, 5th ed., pp. 384–485 (vehicle electrical systems, starter batteries, alternators, starting systems) and pp. 521–523 (terminal designations). Diagrams redrawn; updated for 2026.

Charge balance: alternator vs consumers

An alternator delivers nothing below its 0-ampere speed (about 1,000 alternator rpm), then its current rises steeply and flattens towards a maximum. Because it turns 2.5 to 3 times faster than the crankshaft, idle is the critical point: in city traffic the engine spends much of its time there, and if the consumers draw more than the alternator delivers at idle, the battery drains.

Electrical energy management (EEM)

EEM coordinates generation, storage and consumption. Its basis is battery management: an intelligent battery sensor on the negative terminal measures current, voltage and temperature, and estimates state of charge (SOC), state of health (SOH) and state of function (SOF: "will the battery still crank the engine after this stop?"). From that, the energy coordinator decides whether to raise alternator voltage, raise idle speed, delay or shed comfort loads, or inhibit start/stop.

Starter and engagement relay

Turning the key to start energises terminal 50. The engagement relay (solenoid) has two windings: a strong pull-in winding and a hold-in winding. Together they pull the plunger, which pushes the pinion into the ring gear through the engagement lever and then closes the main contact. Main current (several hundred amps) flows from terminal 30 through the motor. The pull-in winding is now shorted out, and the hold-in winding alone keeps the contact closed. An overrunning clutch stops the running engine from driving the starter.

These numbers label device terminals, not wires, and they still appear in every wiring diagram and in ECU power-mode logic ("KL15 on" means ignition on).

Chapter 06

Chassis & safety systems

Chassis systems decide how much of the available tyre grip turns into braking, traction and cornering force. Safety systems protect the occupants when grip runs out. Almost all of them share the same building blocks: four wheel-speed sensors, a yaw-rate and acceleration cluster, a hydraulic modulator and a fast control loop.

Sources: author's M.Tech course material (Automotive Systems: ABS, TCS, ESP, ACC, ADAS, occupant safety, x-by-wire) and Bosch, Automotive Electrics and Automotive Electronics, 5th ed., pp. 46–69, checked against current regulations. Diagrams redrawn.

Tyre grip and brake slip

A braked wheel turns slightly slower than the car moves. Brake slip λ = (vvehicle − vwheel) ÷ vvehicle: 0 % is a free-rolling wheel, 100 % a locked one. Braking force rises with slip up to a peak, usually between about 8 % and 30 %, then falls. A locked wheel also loses almost all of its side force, which is why a car with locked front wheels cannot be steered.

How the hydraulic modulator controls one wheel

Each wheel brake has an inlet valve (normally open, from the master cylinder) and an outlet valve (normally closed, to a low-pressure accumulator and return pump). The control unit cycles each wheel through three phases several times per second while the driver keeps the pedal down.

PhaseInlet valveOutlet valveReturn pumpWhen
Build (pressure rises)OpenClosed—Normal braking; wheel has grip to spare
HoldClosedClosed—Wheel deceleration reaches the threshold

Modern systems use four channels and four sensors, so every wheel is controlled on its own. Rear wheels are often controlled together on the "select-low" principle, using the wheel with less grip to protect stability. Older or simpler layouts (three-channel, or one-channel rear-axle ABS on early pick-ups) shared valves between wheels.

Traction control and stability control

TCS works the other way round from ABS: when a driven wheel spins during acceleration, it brakes that wheel (acting like a locking differential) and asks the engine or motor to cut torque. ESC (also called ESP or DSC) compares the path the driver wants with the path the car is taking. The wanted yaw rate comes from steering angle and speed; the real one from the yaw-rate sensor. If they differ, ESC brakes a single wheel to create a correcting yaw moment and reduces drive torque.

Driver assistance: from ACC to Level 2+

Adaptive cruise control (ACC) holds a set speed until a slower vehicle appears ahead, then keeps a time gap to it, typically 1 to 2.5 s, using the engine and brakes. The radar measures distance and closing speed directly. The required distance grows with speed: at 100 km/h a 1.8 s gap is 50 m.

Occupant protection

In a frontal impact at about 50 km/h against a hard obstacle, the airbag control unit needs roughly 10 ms to recognise the crash and fire the inflator; the driver airbag then takes around 30 ms more to fill, so it is ready about 40 ms after first contact, before the occupant moves forward into it. Side impacts leave far less room, so side sensing and thorax-bag inflation each have to happen within roughly 5 to 10 ms.

Sensing

A central control unit holds acceleration sensors (and often a roll-rate sensor). Satellite sensors near the front and in the doors or pillars report early over a current-modulated interface (PSI5). A plausibility ("safing") path stops a single sensor fault from firing an airbag.

Actuators

Seat-belt pretensioners and load limiters, front, side, curtain and knee airbags, adaptive (two-stage) inflators, and active bonnets for pedestrian protection. Firing circuits are monitored continuously; a fault lights the airbag warning lamp.

X-by-wire

"By-wire" replaces a mechanical or hydraulic link between driver and actuator with sensors, ECUs and electric actuators. Everything that was implicitly safe in a steel shaft must now be designed in: redundant sensors, two independent power supplies, two communication paths and an ECU architecture that keeps working after one fault (fail-operational).

CAN frame anatomy

CAN arbitration

Any node may start transmitting when the bus is idle. The bus behaves as a wired-AND: a dominant 0 overwrites a recessive 1. Every transmitter reads back each bit; a node that sent recessive but reads dominant has lost and becomes a receiver. The lowest identifier always wins, and the winning frame is never destroyed.

CAN FD frame and its extra error detection

CAN FD keeps the classical arbitration field, then changes the frame between IDE and ACK. Three bits are easy to mix up, so here they are side by side: RRS replaces RTR and is always dominant (CAN FD has no remote frames), FDF marks the frame as CAN FD, and BRS decides whether the data phase switches to the fast bit rate.

Why the stuff count? In classical CAN two bit flips can create one stuff condition and remove another; the receiver then shifts its view of the frame and the CRC can miss it. Counting the dynamic stuff bits, and protecting that count with Gray code and parity, closes that hole. Faults that flip a bit (type A) are caught by the CRC (Hamming distance 6, so up to 5 random bit errors); faults that make a receiver drop or insert a bit through wrong resynchronisation (type B) are caught by the stuff count and the fixed stuff bits.

Error confinement: how CAN isolates a faulty node

Every node keeps two counters: a transmit error counter (TEC) and a receive error counter (REC). Errors push them up, successful frames pull them down. A node that keeps causing errors first loses the right to destroy other nodes' frames (error-passive), and finally disconnects itself (bus-off). The bus stays usable for everyone else.

Bit timing and oscillator tolerance (CAN FD)

Each node builds its bit time from time quanta (TQ) of its own clock: a sync segment of 1 TQ, then phase segment 1 (which includes the propagation segment), the sample point, and phase segment 2. Nodes resynchronise on recessive-to-dominant edges by up to SJW. Because every oscillator is slightly off, the bit timing decides how much clock tolerance the network accepts. CAN FD has two bit timings and switches between them inside the frame, so it needs five conditions instead of classical CAN's two.

Diagnostics: UDS and DoIP

Chapter 08

Software-defined vehicle

In a software-defined vehicle (SDV), features are added and improved through software over the car's life rather than being frozen at the start of production. That needs three changes underneath: fewer, more powerful computers; communication based on services instead of fixed signals; and a hardware abstraction so applications no longer depend on which ECU owns which wire.

Sources: author's M.Tech course material (SDV, SOME/IP, AUTOSAR folders), checked against AUTOSAR PRS SOME/IP and SOME/IP-SD (R24-11), COVESA VSS and vendor zonal-architecture white papers. Several statements in the course slides were corrected; see the project's verification record.

E/E architecture: from boxes to zones

What a zone controller actually does. In production designs the zone controller is a robust microcontroller-class ECU (AURIX, S32 and similar), not a supercomputer. It reads the switches and sensors in its corner of the car, drives the lamps and motors there, distributes and protects 12 V or 48 V power with eFuses, and bridges legacy CAN and LIN to the Ethernet backbone. Heavy computing (ADAS perception, infotainment, the vehicle's application software) runs on one or a few central or high-performance computers.

Signal-based buses do not disappear: control loops and zone-level I/O stay on CAN, CAN FD and LIN. Services connect the computers above them.

SOME/IP

SOME/IP (Scalable service-Oriented MiddlewarE over IP) was introduced by BMW in 2011 and became part of AUTOSAR in release 4.1. It is an application-layer protocol that runs over UDP or TCP on any Ethernet speed. A service is a bundle of methods (request/response or fire-and-forget calls), events (sent to subscribers on change or cyclically) and fields (a value with optional getter, setter and change notifier). Which transport each one uses is configured per service; large UDP messages are split by SOME/IP-TP.

Service discovery (SOME/IP-SD)

Before a client can call a service it has to know where the service runs. SOME/IP-SD messages are themselves SOME/IP messages (Message ID 0xFFFF8100) sent over UDP, by default on port 30490, usually to a multicast group. A server repeatedly offers its services; a client that starts later can find them; then it subscribes to the event groups it wants. Offers and subscriptions carry a time-to-live, so a crashed ECU's services disappear on their own.

DDS: the data-centric alternative

The Data Distribution Service (DDS, an OMG standard) also does publish/subscribe, but around shared topics rather than services: publishers write samples of a typed topic, subscribers read them, and the middleware handles discovery and delivery with no broker. Its strength is a rich set of quality-of-service policies (reliability, history depth, deadline, durability, liveliness) that each reader and writer can set. DDS is the middleware under ROS 2 and is one of the network bindings of AUTOSAR Adaptive alongside SOME/IP.

To decouple applications from the wiring, the vehicle exposes a standard API. ISO 26262 and ASIL

ISO 26262 covers functional safety of E/E systems. In the concept phase a hazard analysis and risk assessment (HARA) rates each hazardous event for Severity, Exposure and Controllability. Safety lifecycle in brief

  1. Item definition — what the function is, its boundaries and interfaces.
  2. HARA — hazardous events, ASIL, and a safety goal for each.
  3. Functional safety concept — functional safety requirements allocated to architecture elements.
  4. Technical safety concept — technical requirements, safety mechanisms, diagnostic coverage.
  5. HW and SW development — ASIL-dependent methods, metrics (SPFM, LFM, PMHF) and verification.

TARA: threat analysis and risk assessment

  1. Asset identification — what needs protecting and which property (confidentiality, integrity, availability) matters.
  2. Damage scenarios — what harm results if the property is lost.
  3. Impact rating — Safety, Financial, Operational, Privacy (SFOP): severe, major, moderate or negligible.
  4. Threat scenarios — how the property could be compromised (STRIDE helps).
  5. Attack path analysis — concrete steps an attacker takes, often drawn as attack trees.
  6. Attack feasibility — rated by attack potential (below).
  7. Risk value — impact × feasibility from a risk matrix, 1 (lowest) to 5.
  8. Risk treatment — reduce, avoid, share or retain; reduction produces cybersecurity goals and requirements.

Secure diagnostics: SecurityAccess (0x27)

Protected services such as writing a VIN or flashing software are locked. The tester asks for a random seed, computes a key with a secret algorithm, and sends it back. Wrong keys are counted; after too many, the ECU enforces a delay to stop brute-force attempts.

Secure onboard communication (SecOC)

CAN has no sender authentication: any node can send any ID. AUTOSAR SecOC appends a freshness value and a truncated MAC (normally AES-128-CMAC) to each protected PDU. The receiver recomputes the MAC with the shared key and rejects frames that are altered or replayed.

  3. Attack potential: time, expertise, knowledge, window of opportunity, equipment.
  4. 0x27: seed → key; NRC 35 invalid key, 36 attempts exceeded, 37 delay not expired.
  5. SecOC: MAC for integrity and authenticity, freshness for replay protection; no confidentiality.
  6. Root of trust in the HSM: secure boot, key storage, crypto acceleration.

Cruise control: from physics to a PI controller

The longitudinal model is Newton's law: M·dv/dt = Fdrive − b·v − c·v² − M·g·sin θ, with the drive force following the throttle command through a first-order lag (time constant τe). The quadratic air drag makes the plant nonlinear. Linearised around a cruising speed v0, it behaves like Ke ÷ ((τes + 1)(Ms + b + 2cv0)): the drag adds 2cv0 of effective damping, so the car responds faster at higher speed. A PI controller removes the steady-state error that a hill would otherwise leave.
